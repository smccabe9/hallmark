How to write an A answer

Before the content, the method. Every short answer is graded the same way.

Prof. Navarro-Farr says an A answer uses proper terms, defines them, gives examples, and is explicit. “Explicit” is the word to remember: she won’t infer anything, so a step you skip is a point you lose. Build every answer from four moves:

  1. Define. Open with the key term and a one-sentence definition.
  2. Explain. Say how it works or why it matters, step by step.
  3. Example. A specific case from lecture, slides, the reading or class activities: blue jeans, atlatls, CWAM, the film.
  4. Connect. End with one sentence tying it to a bigger theme: context, chronology or stewardship.

Lesson 1. Dating and chronology

Archaeologists answer “when?” in two ways: by putting things in order, and by giving an age in years. Covers Q1, Q4 and Q6 (week 6, DTE Ch. 5).

Relative dating: putting things in order

Relative dating tells you whether something is older or younger than something else. It gives an order, never a number of years. It rests on two keys.

Key 1, the law of superposition. In undisturbed deposits, lower layers were laid down first, so they are older than the layers above. Nicolaus Steno first stated it in 1669, for geology. Think of a laundry pile: the shirt at the bottom went in first. The study of these layers (strata) is stratigraphy.

Key 2, the index fossil concept. Layers that contain the same distinctive fossils or artifacts are about the same age, even at different sites. The geologist William Smith noticed this with fossils. Archaeologists use artifacts the same way: a projectile point style made for only a short time is a time-marker (or index artifact).

Superposition orders layers within one site. Index artifacts link layers between sites. Together they build a regional sequence.

The caution that shows depth. Superposition only works if layers aren’t disturbed. Burrowing animals, pits dug by later people or erosion can mix them, so archaeologists check before trusting the sequence. Add the law of cross-cutting relationships: a pit or feature that cuts through a layer is younger than that layer.

Two more terms tie layers to dates. Terminus post quem, “date after which”: a layer can’t be older than the newest thing in it. Terminus ante quem, “date before which”: a layer sealed below something datable is older than it. And in situ means “in place”, still where it was originally left.

Absolute dating: assigning years

Absolute (or chronometric) dating tells you how many years old something is, usually as a range with a ± sign. The methods to know:

  • Radiocarbon (C-14). Living things absorb carbon-14. When they die it decays at a known rate, with a half-life of about 5,730 years, so measuring what’s left tells how long ago the organism died. Works on organic material up to about 50,000 years old.
  • Dendrochronology. Counting and matching annual tree rings; can be accurate to the exact year.
  • Potassium-argon. Dates volcanic rock, for very old sites (millions of years), like early human sites in East Africa.
  • Thermoluminescence. Measures energy trapped in heated objects like pottery since they were last fired.
  • Also: obsidian hydration and historical records.

On the test: Lesson 1

Multiple choice could ask
  • Which principle says lower layers are older? Superposition. Trap: cross-cutting is about pits cutting layers.
  • Who goes with what: Steno is superposition (1669); William Smith is index fossils.
  • Relative or absolute? Seriation is relative, even though it feels like dating.
  • Numbers that get swapped: 5,730 years is the half-life; about 50,000 years is the range.
  • Post vs. ante: post quem is “after which” (newest thing inside); ante quem is “before which” (sealed below).
Short answer Q1

The two keys of relative dating. Define relative dating, explain superposition with a stratified site, explain index fossils with a point style, then the disturbance caution, and end on context.

Short answer Q4

Two radiocarbon problems. One-sentence setup (half-life 5,730 years), then contamination and calibration, each with what goes wrong and the fix.

Short answer Q6

Relative vs. absolute. Define both, two examples each, strengths and weaknesses, then the sentence below.

A sentence to write

“Relative and absolute methods are strongest when combined: superposition orders the layers, and a radiocarbon date from one layer anchors the whole sequence in calendar years.”

Check yourself

A pit cuts through layers 1 and 2. Is the pit older or younger than layer 2?

Younger. By the law of cross-cutting relationships, whatever cuts through a layer came after it.

Why can’t you date a 100,000-year-old site with radiocarbon?

Radiocarbon only works up to about 50,000 years. For very old sites on volcanic rock, potassium-argon is used.

What problem does calibration fix?

Atmospheric carbon-14 levels changed over time, so raw radiocarbon dates are off. Calibration against tree rings converts radiocarbon years to calendar years.

Lesson 2. Classification and seriation

How a pile of objects becomes data, and how that data puts sites in time order. Covers Q2, Q3 and Q5 (week 7, DTE Ch. 6).

Three building blocks, smallest first

An attribute is one observable or measurable feature of an artifact: color, shape, length, decoration, material, how it was made. There are three kinds:

  • Form (morphological): shape and size, like length, width and notches.
  • Stylistic: decoration and color, like painted lines or slip color.
  • Technological: how it was made and from what, like raw material, firing method or flaking technique.

A type is a group of artifacts sharing a consistent cluster of attributes: a projectile point that is always side-notched, about 3 cm long and made of chert. A typology is an organized system of types for a region or artifact class: all the point types of a region, arranged by shape and time period.

Attributes are ingredients. A type is a recipe that always uses the same ingredients. A typology is the whole cookbook.

Types come in three kinds too. Morphological types describe physical form (grouping pots by shape). Temporal types change over time, so they date sites (a point style made only 1000 to 500 BC); these are the time-markers from Lesson 1. Functional types group by use (scrapers vs. projectile points).

Why bother? Types organize huge amounts of data, since a site can yield thousands of artifacts. They help date sites. They allow comparison across a region, to spot trade or shared culture. And they give archaeologists a shared language. One thoughtful point to add: archaeologists create types to answer their own questions, so types may not match how the makers saw their objects.

Seriation: dating by popularity

Seriation is a relative dating method: it orders artifacts in time but gives no calendar years. It works because styles have a life cycle. A style starts rare, becomes popular, then fades out. Graph its popularity over time and the shape is a battleship curve: narrow, wide, narrow.

There are two kinds. Frequency seriation orders by how common each type is: the battleship curves. Stylistic (contextual) seriation orders by gradual changes in style: Flinders Petrie ordered Egyptian graves by changes in pottery handles.

The blue jeans activity was seriation in action. Leg cut, rise, wash and fit were the attributes. Grouping jeans by shared attributes created types, and watching types rise and fall showed seriation. Use your own class notes, but common examples are:

EraAttributes that act as time-markers
1970sBell-bottoms, flared legs, high waist
1980sAcid wash, tapered legs, high waist
1990sBaggy or relaxed fit, light wash
2000sLow-rise, bootcut, distressing
2010sSkinny leg, stretch fabric
2020sHigh-rise again, wide or straight leg: a style came back

The limitation to mention. High-rise jeans returned. Seriation assumes a style appears and fades only once, so a comeback breaks the method.

  • Names to match: Deetz and Dethlefsen with gravestones; Petrie with Egyptian graves and pottery handles.

“Attributes are the raw data; the typology is the finished system that lets us date and compare sites.”

Check yourself

“Scrapers vs. projectile points” is which kind of type?

Functional: it groups artifacts by what they were used for.

Why did the jeans activity reveal a weakness of seriation?

High-rise jeans came back, but seriation assumes each style appears and fades only once.

How does a temporal type connect back to Lesson 1?

A temporal type is a time-marker, the index fossil concept applied to artifacts: finding it dates the layer.

Lesson 3. From objects to behavior

How we get from things in the ground to what people did. Covers Q7 (week 9, DTE Ch. 7).

The archaeological record is static: objects sitting in the ground today. What we want to explain is dynamic: past behavior. Middle-range theory is the research that builds the bridge between the two, through experiments, ethnoarchaeology (studying living people) and careful observation.

Experimental archaeology recreates past tools or actions to test ideas. Replication means making a copy of a tool to learn how it was made or used. Your two class activities are both bridges.

The CWAM close-looking visit

At the College of Wooster Art Museum, slow looking was the method. Insights you could use:

  • Observation vs. inference. Separate what you can see (tool marks, wear, paint traces, repairs) from what you assume (its use or meaning). Record observations first, conclusions second.
  • Objects carry their life history. Use-wear, burning, chips and repairs show how an object was made, used and reused.
  • Museum objects can lack context. Many have little or no provenience, so we can describe them but not fully explain them. This links to Q10.

The atlatl experiment

You measured stride length and throwing distance. Insights you could use:

  • Experiments turn guesses into testable data. Instead of assuming an atlatl adds distance, we measured and compared.
  • Variability matters. Skill, practice and body size changed distances. Past behavior varied too, so one measurement isn’t enough.
  • Sample size and control. Repeated trials and controlled variables come before trusting a pattern.
  • Practice effect. If your second throw improved, learning shaped performance. Ancient hunters trained for years, so our numbers likely underestimate their skill.

On the test: Lesson 3

Multiple choice could ask
  • What is middle-range theory? Research linking what we see today to past behavior.
  • Making a copy of a tool to learn how it was used is replication.
  • Studying living people to understand the past is ethnoarchaeology.
Short answer Q7

One insight from each activity. This asks for your insights, so use your own memory. Open with middle-range theory, then CWAM (what you looked at, your insight, why it matters), then the atlatl (your data, your insight, why it matters).

A sentence to write

“Both activities show that explanations of the past must be grounded in careful, recorded observations.”

Check yourself

Why is the archaeological record called “static”?

It is objects sitting in the ground today, not the living behavior that produced them. Middle-range theory bridges the gap.

Give one reason the atlatl numbers may underestimate ancient hunters.

The practice effect: hunters trained for years, while students threw only a few times.

Lesson 4. Heritage law: NAGPRA and CRM

US law grew from protecting federal land (1906), to planned management (1966), to Native American rights (1990). Covers Q8 and Q9 (week 10, DTE Ch. 13).

Cultural resource management (CRM)

CRM is the management and protection of archaeological and historic sites, usually when construction or development threatens them. Most US archaeology today is done in CRM, not universities.

Early efforts were mostly two things: stopping looting on federal land, and salvage archaeology, rushing to dig sites right before dams and roads destroyed them. Learn this timeline in order:

YearLaw or programWhat it did
1906Antiquities ActFirst US protection law: protected sites on federal land, required permits to dig, let presidents create national monuments. Aimed at looting in the Southwest
1930sNew Deal relief archaeology (WPA, TVA)Unemployed workers on large excavations, especially before dams flooded valleys
1935Historic Sites ActMade preserving historic places national policy
1946 to 1960sRiver Basin Surveys (Smithsonian)Salvage archaeology ahead of big postwar dam projects
1960Reservoir Salvage ActRequired recovering data before reservoir flooding
1966National Historic Preservation Act (NHPA)The turning point: National Register of Historic Places, State Historic Preservation Offices, Section 106 review
1969National Environmental Policy Act (NEPA)Impact studies, including cultural resources, for federal projects
1974Archaeological and Historic Preservation Act (Moss-Bennett)Agencies may spend up to 1% of project funds on archaeology
1979Archaeological Resources Protection Act (ARPA)Tougher fines and jail time for looting on public land
1990NAGPRANative American rights and repatriation

How CRM changed. From salvage to planning: after NHPA, Section 106 makes federal projects consider historic sites in advance. Preservation in place is now often preferred, with excavation as mitigation (reducing damage) when avoiding a site isn’t possible. A profession grew: CRM firms, government archaeologists, contract work. And since NAGPRA, descendant communities are consulted. The arc is from salvage to stewardship: caring for the past on behalf of everyone.

NAGPRA (1990)

The Native American Graves Protection and Repatriation Act is a federal human rights law. Its core idea: Native American human remains and certain objects are not just scientific specimens. Lineal descendants and tribes have legal rights to them.

It covers four categories: human remains; funerary objects (placed with burials); sacred objects (needed for ongoing religious practice); and objects of cultural patrimony (belonging to the whole community, not one person).

The stipulations, in three verbs: inventory, consult, repatriate. Federal agencies and federally funded museums must inventory covered items, consult with tribes and descendants, and return them on request. Repatriation means returning remains or objects to the people they came from. Excavations on federal or tribal land require consultation, discoveries must be reported, and trafficking is illegal.

How it changed archaeology. Collaboration replaced control, leading to community-based and Indigenous archaeology. Oral traditions now count as evidence alongside science. Museums returned collections, and updated rules in 2024 strengthened tribal consent, so several major museums closed or covered exhibits.

The example: Kennewick Man (“the Ancient One”), about 9,000 years old, found in Washington in 1996. A long legal fight between scientists and tribes followed. DNA later linked him to Native Americans, and tribes reburied him in 2017.

Lesson 5. The ethics of looting

Who should hold the past, and what to do with objects torn from it. Covers Q10 and Q11 (week 11, Ethics Bowl and the film).

There is no right or wrong answer here. You are graded on a clear position, good reasons, examples, and showing you understand the other side. Use this formula:

  1. Take a clear stance in your first sentence (“I believe…”).
  2. Give two reasons, each with an example (the film, a law, a class discussion).
  3. Acknowledge the strongest counterargument, then explain why you still hold your view.
  4. Add a nuance (“It depends on…”). This shows mature thinking.

The key words

Provenience is the exact 3-D spot where an artifact was found. Provenance is its ownership history after discovery. An object pulled out of the ground by looters is decontextualized: removed from its setting and stripped of its information. Antiquities are ancient objects, especially ones bought and sold. The public trust means museum holdings are kept for public benefit, not private gain.

Q10: Should looted artifacts be studied and published?

Out of the Maya Tombs is about painted Maya vessels looted from tombs and sold on the antiquities market. Many carry hieroglyphic texts and scenes that helped scholars decipher Maya writing, but their findspots were destroyed.

SideMain argumentSupporting points
Yes, study themIgnoring information is itself unethical; the damage is already doneThe texts and images still teach us about Maya religion, politics and writing; refusing to look doesn’t un-loot the object
No, don’t publishWithout context an artifact loses most of its meaning; publishing raises its market valueExpert publication can authenticate pieces and encourage more looting; the tomb, the burial and the associations are lost forever
Middle groundStudy, but with conditionsPublish with clear notes that they were looted and lack provenience, and never authenticate or appraise for sale; or study only once in a public museum or returned to the country of origin (Guatemala, Mexico)

The key point, whichever side you take: looting destroys context (the tomb, the body, the other offerings), and context is what tells us who owned an object and how it was used. Tie it back to CWAM if you noticed how little some museum pieces could tell you.

Q11: Private collections vs. the public trust

State the background clearly. The 1970 UNESCO Convention set international rules against illegally exporting and trading cultural property. The 2008 AAMD guidelines say member museums should only acquire objects documented as leaving their country of origin before 1970, or legally exported after. Many objects without that paperwork sit in private collections, unseen.

PositionMain argumentSupporting points
Museums should accept themPublic access beats a hidden private shelfObjects can be studied, conserved, displayed and potentially returned; museums can research their history openly
Museums should refuse themAccepting rewards the illegal marketIt “launders” looted objects and tells collectors buying looted items pays off (tax deductions, prestige); it weakens the 1970 rule

Does it depend on the type of object? The question asks this outright, so answer it.

  • Human remains and sacred objects: most argue these should go back to descendant communities, not into any collection. That is NAGPRA’s logic.
  • Unique, high-value pieces like painted Maya vases: more likely looted to order, so accepting them creates more risk.
  • Common, mass-produced items like ancient coins: lower risk of encouraging new looting, and more scholarly value when grouped together.

Middle ground: accept the object but publish everything known about its history, actively offer return to the country of origin, or hold it as a loan rather than an acquisition.

On the test: Lesson 5

Multiple choice could ask
  • The classic trap: provenience = find spot, provenance = ownership history.
  • Years: UNESCO 1970; AAMD 2008, which uses 1970 as its cutoff.
  • Why did the looted Maya vessels matter? Their texts helped decipher Maya writing.
Short answer Q10

A stance on studying looted artifacts, two reasons with examples from the film, the counterargument, a middle ground, and context as the key point.

Short answer Q11

A stance on museums accepting undocumented objects, the UNESCO and AAMD background, reasons and counterargument, and an explicit answer to “does it depend on the object?”

A sentence to write

“Whatever we decide about a looted object, the information lost with its context can never be recovered, which is why stewardship has to start before the looting does.”

Check yourself

A museum record says a vase was “sold at auction in 1985”. Is that provenience or provenance?

Provenance: ownership history. Provenience would be where in the ground it was found.

Why might expert publication encourage looting?

Publication can authenticate a piece and raise its market value, which rewards the looters and dealers.

What does Q11 explicitly ask you to address?

Whether your answer depends on the type of object: human remains and sacred objects, unique high-value pieces, or common items like coins.
